# Opening Leads

## Overview

The **opening lead** is the first card played by the defense and often determines the outcome of the contract. A successful opening lead can establish defensive tricks, disrupt declarer's communications, or prevent declarer from developing extra winners. Conversely, an inaccurate lead may immediately give declarer an extra trick.

Choosing the correct opening lead requires evaluating:

- The auction.
- The contract (Suit or Notrump).
- Partner's bidding.
- Your own holding.
- Partnership lead agreements.

Expert defenders treat the opening lead as one of the most important decisions in bridge.

---

# Objectives

The opening lead should aim to:

- Establish defensive winners.
- Develop your longest and strongest suit.
- Help partner's hand.
- Attack declarer's weaknesses.
- Avoid giving away unnecessary tricks.
- Set the tone for the defense.

---

# Factors to Consider

Before selecting an opening lead, ask:

- Is the contract **Suit** or **Notrump**?
- What did the auction reveal?
- Did partner bid a suit?
- Does declarer have a known weak suit?
- Is an active or passive defense appropriate?

---

# Opening Leads Against Notrump Contracts

### General Strategy

Against Notrump contracts, defenders usually want to:

- Establish long-suit winners.
- Force declarer to lose control of a suit.
- Develop tricks before declarer establishes theirs.

### Recommended Leads

- Fourth-highest from the longest and strongest suit.
- Partner's bid suit.
- Top of a sequence.
- Interior sequence (by partnership agreement).

### Avoid

- Unsupported Aces.
- Unsupported Kings.
- Leading away from unsupported Queens.

---

# Opening Leads Against Suit Contracts

### General Strategy

Against suit contracts, defenders often seek to:

- Prevent declarer from ruffing losers.
- Promote trump tricks.
- Establish side-suit winners.
- Avoid giving declarer a free finesse.

### Recommended Leads

- Partner's bid suit.
- Singleton (when a ruff is likely).
- Trump (to reduce declarer's ruffing opportunities).
- Top of a sequence.

### Avoid

- Leading away from unsupported honors.
- Leading into declarer's announced strength.

---

# Standard Lead Agreements

The exact agreements vary by partnership.

## From Honor Sequences

| Holding | Standard Lead |
|---------|---------------|
| AKQ | A |
| KQJ | K |
| QJ10 | Q |
| J109 | J |

Lead the **top of the sequence**.

---

## Interior Sequences

| Holding | Standard Lead |
|---------|---------------|
| KJ10 | J |
| Q109 | 10 |
| AJ10 | J (partnership agreement) |

Many partnerships lead the top of the interior sequence.

---

## From Honor Combinations

| Holding | Typical Lead |
|---------|--------------|
| AKx | A |
| KQx | K |
| QJx | Q |
| J10x | J |

---

## From Small Cards

Against Notrump:

Lead **fourth-highest**.

Example:

```
♠ K8732

Lead:
♠3
```

Against Suit Contracts:

Partnership agreements vary:

- Third-highest.
- Low from three small.
- MUD (Middle-Up-Down).
- Fourth-best.

---

# Leading Partner's Suit

When partner has bid a suit, leading that suit is often correct.

General principles:

- Lead top from touching honors.
- Lead low from unsupported length.
- Follow partnership agreements.

---

# Singleton Leads

Singleton leads are common against suit contracts when:

- Dummy is expected to hold trump support.
- Partner is likely to gain the lead.
- A ruff may be available.

Avoid singleton leads against Notrump contracts.

---

# Trump Leads

Trump leads are effective when:

- Declarer has shown a two-suited hand.
- Dummy is likely to ruff losers.
- The auction suggests a cross-ruff.

Trump leads reduce declarer's ruffing opportunities.

---

# Active vs. Passive Leads

## Active Lead

Purpose:

- Establish tricks immediately.
- Attack declarer's weak suits.

Examples:

- Leading a long suit against Notrump.
- Leading partner's suit.

---

## Passive Lead

Purpose:

- Avoid giving declarer a trick.
- Wait for declarer to commit.

Examples:

- Safe trump lead.
- Small card from a weak suit.
- Avoid leading away from unsupported honors.

# Common Mistakes

- Leading away from unsupported honors.
- Ignoring the auction.
- Forgetting partner's bid.
- Giving declarer a free finesse.
- Leading aggressively when passive defense is appropriate.

---

# Defensive Planning After the Lead

After dummy appears:

- Reevaluate the defense.
- Count declarer's winners.
- Count declarer's likely losers.
- Identify entry positions.
- Decide whether to continue or switch suits.

---

# Partnership Agreements

Discuss:

- Fourth-best or third/fifth leads.
- Top of sequences.
- Interior sequences.
- Rusinow leads (if played).
- Attitude leads.
- Lead-directing doubles.
- Opening leads from A-K combinations.
- Trump lead style.

---

# Advantages of Good Opening Leads

- Establish immediate defensive tricks.
- Give partner valuable information.
- Set up long suits.
- Prevent declarer's plan.
- Improve defensive communication.

---

# Disadvantages of Poor Opening Leads

- Give declarer an extra trick.
- Reveal unnecessary information.
- Destroy defensive communication.
- Lose tempo.
- Turn making contracts into overtricks.

# Defender's Checklist

Before making the opening lead:

- What does the auction tell me?
- Did partner bid a suit?
- Is this a suit or notrump contract?
- Should I attack or defend passively?
- Which lead gives declarer the fewest free tricks?

---

# Memory Aid

**Opening Lead = "Plan Before You Play."**

Remember:

- **Use the auction as your guide.**
- **Against Notrump, attack long suits.**
- **Against suit contracts, consider passive leads, trump leads, or singleton leads.**
- **Lead partner's suit whenever appropriate.**
- **Follow your partnership's lead agreements consistently.**

The opening lead is the defense's only opportunity to act before declarer begins executing a plan. Expert defenders combine information from the auction, partnership agreements, and card holdings to choose the lead that gives the defense the greatest chance of defeating the contract.
