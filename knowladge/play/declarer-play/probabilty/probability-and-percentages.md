# Probability and Percentages

## Overview

**Probability and Percentages** are fundamental concepts in bridge. Every important decision—whether to finesse, play for the drop, establish a suit, or choose between competing lines of play—should be based on **the line with the highest probability of success**, not on hope or intuition.

Expert players constantly evaluate the odds using:

- Mathematics
- The auction
- Distribution
- High Card Point (HCP) count
- Restricted Choice
- Vacant Places
- Inference

The guiding principle is:

> **Bridge is a game of probabilities, refined by information.**

---

# Purpose

Understanding probabilities allows players to:

- Choose the best line of play.
- Compare alternative strategies.
- Reduce guesswork.
- Improve declarer play.
- Improve defensive decisions.
- Maximize long-term success.

---

# Basic Principle

Before making any important decision, ask:

- Which line has the highest percentage?
- Has the auction changed the odds?
- Has the play revealed additional information?
- Is there a safer alternative?

The mathematically best play is called the **percentage play**.

---

# Pure Probability vs. Informed Probability

## Pure Probability

Based only on mathematics.

Example:

Missing Queen.

With no additional information:

```
50% chance with West
50% chance with East
```

---

## Informed Probability

Uses additional information such as:

- Auction
- Distribution
- HCP count
- Opening lead
- Signals
- Previous tricks

This information often changes the odds dramatically.

---

# Common Bridge Probabilities

## Missing One Card

A missing honor is equally likely to be held by either opponent.

```
50% / 50%
```

Before additional information is available.

# Example 1 – Finesse or Drop?

Missing:

```
♠K
```

Holding:

```
AQJ
```

Without additional information:

A finesse succeeds:

```
50%
```

Playing for the drop:

```
Approximately 0%
```

(Unless there are only one or two missing spades.)

The finesse is clearly superior.

---

# Example 2 – Eight-Card Fit

Holding:

```
Eight-card trump fit
```

Missing:

```
Five trumps
```

Best assumption:

```
3–2 split

≈68%
```

Draw trumps normally.

---

# Example 3 – Nine-Card Fit

Holding:

```
Nine-card trump fit
```

Missing:

```
Four trumps
```

Most likely:

```
3–1 split

≈50%
```

However:

```
2–2 split

≈40%
```

Planning should consider both possibilities.

---

# Probability Changes During Play

As information appears, probabilities change.

Examples:

- The auction suggests West has more HCP.
- West shows out of a suit.
- East falsecards.
- A defender reveals a long suit.

Expert players continuously revise their estimates.

---

# Combining Probability with Counting

Never rely on probability alone.

Combine it with:

- Counting Distribution.
- Counting High Card Points.
- Restricted Choice.
- Vacant Places.
- Inference.

The more information available, the less important pure probability becomes.

---

# Percentage Plays

A **percentage play** is the line that succeeds most often over many deals.

Example:

Choosing between:

- A 68% line.
- A 50% line.

The 68% line is normally correct, even if it fails on this particular hand.

Bridge rewards correct decisions over time, not short-term results.

---

# Planning the Play

Before making a key decision, ask:

- What are the mathematical odds?
- Has the auction changed them?
- Have I counted the distribution?
- Have I counted the HCP?
- Which line succeeds most often?

---

# Common Situations

Probability is especially important:

- Finesses.
- Playing for the drop.
- Suit combinations.
- Safety Plays.
- Slam play.
- Endgame decisions.
- Opening leads.
- Defensive switches.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Ignoring the auction.
- Guessing instead of counting.
- Choosing a lower-percentage line unnecessarily.
- Forgetting Restricted Choice.
- Ignoring Vacant Places.

---

# Advantages

- Produces consistent results.
- Reduces guesswork.
- Supports logical planning.
- Improves both declarer play and defense.
- Essential for expert bridge.

---

# Disadvantages

- Probabilities are not guarantees.
- New information changes the odds.
- Sometimes matchpoint or IMP strategy justifies a different choice.
- Requires continuous analysis.

# Player's Checklist

Before choosing a line:

- What is the mathematical probability?

- Have I counted the distribution?
- Have I counted the HCP?
- Which play wins most often over time?

---

# Memory Guide

## Six Steps

1. Calculate the basic probability.
2. Count the distribution.
3. Count the HCP.
4. Apply Restricted Choice or Vacant Places if appropriate.
5. Draw logical inferences.
6. Choose the highest-percentage line.

---

# Comparison

|----------|-----------|

| Percentage Play | The line with the highest chance of success |
| Restricted Choice | Adjust probabilities after an honor appears |
| Vacant Places | Missing cards favor the hand with more unknown cards |
| Inference | Modify probabilities using all available information |

# Memory Aid

**Probability = "Play the Odds, Not Your Hopes."**

Remember:

- **Bridge is a game of percentages, not certainties.**
- **Always begin with mathematical probability, then refine it using information from the auction and play.**
- **The best play is the one that succeeds most often over many deals, not necessarily on a single hand.**
- **As more cards are revealed, continually update your assessment of the odds.**
- **Expert players combine probability, counting, and inference to make logical, high-percentage decisions.**

Probability and Percentages form the mathematical foundation of expert bridge. Every finesse, safety play, trump decision, and defensive switch should be guided by the line that offers the greatest chance of success after considering both pure probability and the information revealed by the auction and the play. Over time, consistently choosing the highest-percentage action is one of the keys to becoming a successful bridge player.
