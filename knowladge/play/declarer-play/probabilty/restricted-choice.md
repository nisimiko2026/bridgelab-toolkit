````md id="restrictedchoice01"
# Restricted Choice

## Overview

**Restricted Choice** is one of the most important probability principles in bridge. It states that **when an opponent plays one of two equivalent cards (such as touching honors), it becomes less likely that they also hold the other equivalent card**.

The reason is simple: if a defender held **both** equivalent cards, they could have played either one. If they held **only one**, they had no choice. Therefore, seeing one honor appear makes it statistically more likely that the other honor is with the opposite defender.

Restricted Choice helps declarers make better decisions in:

- Finesses
- Honor combinations
- Suit development
- Endgame play

The guiding principle is:

> **When a defender had no choice but to play an honor, that play provides valuable information.**

---

# Purpose

Restricted Choice allows players to:

- Improve finesse decisions.
- Make better probability-based plays.
- Locate missing honors.
- Reduce guesswork.
- Increase the chances of making the contract.

---

# Basic Principle

Suppose two touching honors are missing:

```
K Q
```

You lead toward:

```
A J 10
```

West plays the:

```
Q
```

Should you later finesse East for the King or play West for both honors?

Restricted Choice says:

**East is more likely to hold the King.**

If West had **KQ**, West could have played either honor.

If West had **Q only**, West had no alternative.

Therefore, the appearance of the Queen makes it more likely that West did **not** also hold the King.

---

# Why It Works

Without additional information:

Both layouts are equally possible.

After one honor appears:

- Holding **Q alone** forces West to play the Queen.
- Holding **KQ** allows West to choose either honor.

Because one layout allows **two possible plays** and the other only **one**, the "single-honor" layout becomes more likely after observing the actual card played.

---

# Classic Example

Missing honors:

```
K Q
```

Your holding:

```
A J 10 x
```

West plays:

```
Q
```

Correct play:

Assume East is more likely to hold the King.

On the next round, finesse East for the King.

Missing:

Holding:

```

# Example 3 – Touching Intermediate Honors

Missing:

```
10 9
```

Holding:

```
A J 8
```

West plays the:

```
9
```

East is now slightly more likely to hold the 10.

The same principle applies to equivalent intermediate cards.

---

# When Restricted Choice Applies

Restricted Choice is most useful when:

- Two or more **equivalent** cards are missing.
- A defender plays one of those cards.
- The play was likely a **free choice**.
- No bidding or card-play information overrides the probability.

Typical honor combinations include:

```
KQ
QJ
J10
109
```

---

# When It Does NOT Apply

Restricted Choice may not apply when:

- The defender was **forced** to play a particular honor.
- Partnership agreements dictate a specific honor play.
- The auction strongly indicates one defender has the missing honors.
- The defender's play is clearly deceptive (false carding).

Always combine Restricted Choice with other available information.

---

# Planning the Play

Before relying on Restricted Choice, ask:

- Were the honors equivalent?
- Did the defender have a genuine choice?
- Has the auction suggested a different layout?
- Have previous plays revealed additional information?
- Is there a safer percentage line?

---

# Combining with Other Information

Restricted Choice should never be used in isolation.

Combine it with:

- High Card Point counting.
- Distribution counting.
- The auction.
- Opening lead analysis.
- Discovery Play.

These factors may outweigh the Restricted Choice inference.

---

# Common Situations

Restricted Choice frequently arises:

- During finesses.
- In honor combinations.
- While establishing long suits.
- In slam play.
- In suit combinations requiring a guess.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Applying the principle when the defender had no real choice.
- Ignoring the auction.
- Forgetting partnership lead agreements.
- Overlooking false carding.
- Treating Restricted Choice as an absolute rule instead of a probability.

---

# Advantages

- Improves percentage play.
- Reduces guesswork.
- Based on sound probability.
- Useful in many common suit combinations.
- Essential knowledge for advanced declarer play.

---

# Disadvantages

- It is a probability, not a certainty.
- Can be defeated by deceptive carding.
- May be overridden by bidding or distribution.
- Requires careful judgment.

4. Adjust the probability accordingly.

# Memory Aid

**Restricted Choice = "One Honor Seen, Its Partner Is Less Likely Beside It."**

Remember:

- **Restricted Choice is a probability principle, not a guarantee.**
- **It applies only when the defender had a genuine choice of equivalent cards.**
- **Always combine it with information from the auction, counting, and previous play.**
- **Do not let Restricted Choice override clear evidence from the hand.**
- **Expert players use Restricted Choice as one tool in a complete logical analysis.**

Restricted Choice is one of the cornerstones of expert bridge technique. By understanding that the appearance of one equivalent honor changes the probability of where its companion lies, declarers can make more accurate finesse decisions and choose higher-percentage lines of play. Used together with counting and careful observation, it transforms difficult guesses into sound, logical decisions.
````
