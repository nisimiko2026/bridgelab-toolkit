# Dangerous Opponent

## Overview

The **Dangerous Opponent** (also called the **Danger Hand**) is the defender whom declarer wants to **avoid giving the lead**. If that defender gains the lead, they are likely to make a damaging return that defeats the contract.

Recognizing the dangerous opponent is one of the most important skills in notrump play. It often determines whether declarer should take a finesse, duck a trick, or choose one line of play over another.

Although the concept can arise in suit contracts, it is **most important in notrump contracts**, where defenders frequently establish and cash long suits.

---

# Why It Matters

When a defender gains the lead, they may be able to:

- Cash an established long suit.
- Lead through a vulnerable honor.
- Return partner's established suit.
- Defeat the contract before declarer establishes enough winners.

Whenever possible, declarer should choose a line that keeps the dangerous opponent off lead.

---

# Identifying the Dangerous Opponent

Before playing the first trick, ask:

- Which defender has the dangerous long suit?
- Which defender made the opening lead?
- Which defender is likely to hold the missing honors?
- Which defender can reach partner's winners?
- Which defender is likely to make the most damaging return?

The answers often determine the correct line of play.

---

# Example 1 – Avoiding the Opening Leader

Contract:

```
3NT
```

West leads:

```
♠K
```

Dummy:

```
♠ A 7 4
```

Declarer:

```
♠ Q 8 3
```

West has probably led from a long spade suit.

If West regains the lead later, the remaining spades may defeat the contract.

West is therefore the dangerous opponent.

Declarer should consider a **hold-up play** to break the defenders' communication.

---

# Example 2 – Choosing the Correct Finesse

Declarer needs a club finesse.

The finesse can be taken through either defender.

If losing the finesse to West allows West to cash an established suit, but losing to East is harmless, declarer should finesse through **East**, even if both finesses have the same mathematical chance of success.

Avoiding the dangerous opponent is more important than following a purely symmetrical line.

---

# Example 3 – Two Possible Suits

Declarer can establish:

- Clubs
- Diamonds

Developing clubs requires losing the lead to West.

Developing diamonds requires losing the lead to East.

If West is dangerous because of an established spade suit, declarer should prefer the diamond line.

---

# Hold-Up Play

The classic application of the dangerous-opponent principle is the **hold-up play**.

By ducking the defenders' long suit:

- The dangerous opponent exhausts that suit.
- Communication between the defenders is broken.
- When the dangerous opponent later gains the lead, there are no remaining winners to cash.

---

# The Auction Provides Clues

The auction often identifies the dangerous opponent.

Examples:

- An overcall suggests length in a suit.
- A preempt suggests a long suit.
- A takeout double suggests shortness in opener's suit.
- A notrump opening suggests balanced distribution.

Use the auction to estimate which defender is most dangerous.

---

# Reading the Opening Lead

The opening lead frequently reveals:

- A long suit.
- An honor sequence.
- Partnership lead agreements.

Combined with the auction, the opening lead often identifies the defender declarer should avoid.

---

# Entries Matter

A defender is dangerous only if they can:

- Gain the lead.
- Cash winners.
- Reach partner's established suit.

When planning the play, consider not only **who** is dangerous, but also **how** that defender can regain the lead.

---

# Combining Probability and Danger

Sometimes two lines have similar percentages.

Choose the one that avoids the dangerous opponent.

For example:

- Two equivalent finesses.
- Two possible suits to establish.
- Two possible defenders to lose a trick to.

The safer line is often the better practical choice.

---

# Common Situations

The dangerous-opponent principle frequently influences:

- Hold-up plays.
- Finesses.
- Ducking.
- Establishing long suits.
- Entry management.
- Timing decisions.

It is one of the most frequently applied concepts in expert notrump play.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Ignoring which defender has the established long suit.
- Taking the mathematically correct finesse through the wrong defender.
- Winning too early instead of executing a hold-up play.
- Failing to consider which defender can regain the lead.
- Looking only at card combinations instead of the overall position.

# Key Principles

- The dangerous opponent is the defender declarer most wants to keep off lead.
- Identify the dangerous opponent before committing to a line of play.
- Hold-up plays are the classic method of neutralizing a dangerous opponent.
- When several lines have similar percentages, prefer the one that avoids giving the lead to the dangerous opponent.
- Combine information from the auction, opening lead, and card play to identify the dangerous defender accurately.
