# Tempo

## Overview

**Tempo** is one of the most fundamental strategic concepts in bridge. It refers to **the timing and order in which tricks are played**, and whether a player has enough time to develop winners before the opponents can cash theirs.

Unlike a specific technique such as a finesse or squeeze, **tempo is a strategic resource** that influences nearly every aspect of bidding, declarer play, and defense.

Expert players constantly ask:

> **"Who is winning the race?"**

Many contracts are won or lost because one side gains—or loses—just **one trick of tempo**.

---

# Purpose

Understanding tempo allows players to:

- Develop winners before opponents do.
- Maintain the initiative.
- Prevent opponents from establishing suits.
- Preserve communication.
- Improve timing in declarer play and defense.
- Recognize when immediate action is required.

---

# Basic Principle

The guiding principle is:

> **Every trick you give away gives the opponents time.**

Sometimes declarer must:

- Lose a trick early.
- Delay cashing winners.
- Duck intentionally.

Sometimes defenders must:

- Cash winners immediately.
- Switch suits quickly.
- Attack before declarer is ready.

Managing tempo is often the key to making or defeating the contract.

---

# Tempo in Notrump Contracts

Tempo is especially critical in Notrump.

Both sides are often racing to establish long suits.

Example:

```
Declarer:
Needs one club trick.

Defense:
Has five spade winners.
```

If declarer establishes clubs first, the contract succeeds.

If the defense cashes spades first, the contract fails.

One trick of tempo determines the result.

---

# Tempo in Suit Contracts

In suit contracts, tempo often determines:

- Whether declarer can ruff losers.
- Whether defenders can force declarer.
- Whether trumps should be drawn immediately.
- Whether Cross Ruffs succeed.

---

# Example 1 – Declarer Wins the Race

Contract

```
3NT
```

Declarer has:

```
8 winners
```

Needs:

```
1 club winner
```

Defense has:

```
5 immediate spade tricks
```

Declarer wins the opening lead immediately and develops clubs.

The extra club trick arrives before the defense regains the lead.

Declarer wins the race.

---

# Example 2 – Defense Wins the Race

Same contract.

Declarer unnecessarily cashes side winners first.

The delay costs a tempo.

When the defense regains the lead, it cashes five spade tricks.

The contract fails.

---

# Example 3 – Suit Contract

Contract

```
4♠
```

Declarer can:

- Ruff a losing club.

If declarer draws trumps first, the opportunity disappears.

Correct timing preserves the necessary tempo.

---

# Tempo and Hold-Up Play

A Hold-Up Play intentionally **gives up tempo** in one suit to **gain tempo overall**.

By ducking, declarer:

- Cuts defensive communication.
- Prevents defenders from enjoying their long suit later.

The temporary loss creates a long-term gain.

---

# Tempo and Entry Management

Poor entry management often costs tempo.

Example:

Declarer establishes clubs but has no entry to cash them.

The lost entry means the established tricks cannot be reached.

Tempo has been wasted.

---

# Tempo and Safety Plays

A Safety Play sometimes sacrifices one possible overtrick to preserve tempo and maximize the chance of making the contract.

---

# Tempo and Defense

Defenders frequently gain tempo by:

- Switching immediately.
- Cashing winners before declarer establishes a suit.
- Leading trumps.
- Forcing declarer to ruff.

Every successful switch is often a tempo gain.

---

# Tempo and Squeezes

Many squeezes depend on perfect timing.

Declarer must:

- Rectify the count.
- Preserve entries.
- Cash winners in the correct order.

Losing a tempo may destroy the squeeze.

---

# Tempo and Endplays

An endplay often requires declarer to:

- Delay winning a trick.
- Strip side suits.
- Force a defender onto lead at exactly the right moment.

Again, timing is everything.

---

# Planning the Play

Before Trick One, ask:

- Who is ahead in the race?
- How many tricks do I already have?
- Which winners must be developed?
- Can the opponents cash their winners first?
- Where is tempo most valuable?

---

# Common Situations

Tempo is critical in:

- Notrump contracts.
- Long-suit establishment.
- Cross Ruffs.
- Dummy Reversals.
- Hold-Up Plays.
- Safety Plays.
- Squeezes.
- Endplays.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Playing winners too early.
- Delaying necessary development.
- Drawing trumps too soon.
- Ignoring defenders' immediate winners.
- Losing communication.
- Thinking only about tricks instead of timing.

---

# Advantages

Good tempo management:

- Wins races.
- Preserves communication.
- Improves planning.
- Supports advanced techniques.
- Makes difficult contracts easier.

---

# Disadvantages

Poor tempo management:

- Gives opponents extra time.
- Loses entries.
- Prevents suit establishment.
- Destroys squeeze positions.
- Creates unnecessary losers.

# Player's Checklist

Before every critical play:

- Who is winning the race?
- Can I afford to lose the lead?
- Do I need to establish winners immediately?
- Are my entries preserved?
- Will delaying this trick cost a tempo?

# Memory Aid

**Tempo = "Time Is a Trick."**

Remember:

- **Bridge is often a race, not just a counting game.**
- **Every unnecessary play may cost a valuable tempo.**
- **Develop your winners before the opponents develop theirs.**
- **Preserve communication so that established winners can be reached.**
- **Many expert techniques succeed or fail because of one trick of tempo.**

Tempo is one of the invisible resources of bridge. While it cannot be counted like points or tricks, it determines whether winners are established in time, whether entries survive, and whether advanced techniques such as squeezes, endplays, and safety plays succeed. Expert players constantly evaluate tempo, understanding that in many contracts, **the side that controls the timing controls the result.**
