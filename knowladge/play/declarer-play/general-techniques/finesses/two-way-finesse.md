# Two-Way Finesse

## Overview

A **Two-Way Finesse** is a finesse in which declarer can choose **which defender to finesse against**. Unlike a standard finesse, where the missing honor must be in one particular hand, a two-way finesse allows declarer to decide whether to play one opponent for the missing honor or the other.

The decision should rarely be made at random. Expert declarers use information from the auction, opening lead, card play, and probability to determine which opponent is more likely to hold the missing honor.

---

# Objectives

A two-way finesse aims to:

- Locate a missing honor.
- Maximize the probability of success.
- Use available information to choose the better line.
- Avoid the dangerous opponent when possible.

---

# Basic Principle

Suppose declarer holds:

```
Dummy
♠ A J 8

Declarer
♠ K 10 7
```

Missing:

```
♠ Q
```

Declarer has two choices:

- Lead toward the jack, playing West for the queen.
- Cross to the other hand and lead toward the ten, playing East for the queen.

Both lines are possible.

The question is **which opponent is more likely to hold the queen?**

---

# Choosing the Correct Direction

The decision should consider:

- The auction.
- The opening lead.
- Revealed distribution.
- Restricted Choice.
- Vacant Spaces.
- The dangerous opponent.

Expert players rarely rely on pure 50–50 odds.

---

# Information from the Auction

The bidding often provides valuable clues.

Example:

West opens:

```
1♠
```

West is more likely to hold:

- Spade length.
- Spade honors.

Playing West for the missing queen may therefore be the better percentage play.

---

# Information from Distribution

Suppose West has already shown:

- Six clubs.
- Four hearts.

East now has more unknown cards.

According to the **Vacant Spaces Principle**, East becomes more likely to hold the missing queen.

---

# Avoiding the Dangerous Opponent

Sometimes both finesses have similar mathematical chances.

However, one defender is dangerous.

Example:

If losing to West allows West to cash an established suit, while losing to East is harmless, declarer should finesse through East.

The practical percentage is higher because the dangerous opponent remains off lead.

---

# Example 1

Contract:

```
3NT
```

Dummy:

```
♣ A J 8
```

Declarer:

```
♣ K 10 7
```

Missing:

```
♣Q
```

West opened the bidding.

Declarer chooses the finesse through West.

---

# Example 2

The auction is silent.

West has shown six hearts during the play.

East therefore has more vacant spaces.

Declarer chooses the finesse through East.

---

# Example 3

West led a long spade suit.

If West wins a finesse, the defense can cash several spade tricks.

Even though either finesse has similar odds, declarer chooses the finesse through East to avoid the dangerous opponent.

---

# Two-Way vs. One-Way Finesse

| One-Way Finesse | Two-Way Finesse |
|-----------------|-----------------|
| Only one possible direction | Declarer may finesse either opponent |
| Decision is forced | Decision depends on available information |
| Usually about technique | Usually about inference and probability |

---

# Planning the Play

Before taking a two-way finesse, ask:

- What has the auction revealed?
- What does the opening lead suggest?
- Which defender has more vacant spaces?
- Is Restricted Choice relevant?
- Which defender is dangerous?
- Which line offers the best practical chance?

---

# Common Mistakes

- Assuming the choice is always 50–50.
- Ignoring clues from the auction.
- Forgetting to count the defenders' distribution.
- Finessing through the dangerous opponent unnecessarily.
- Taking the finesse before gathering available information.

# Key Principles

- A two-way finesse allows declarer to choose which opponent to finesse against.
- The decision should be based on inference rather than guesswork.
- Use information from the auction, opening lead, distribution, and probability.
- When possible, avoid losing the lead to the dangerous opponent.
- Good declarers postpone the finesse until they have collected as much information as possible.
