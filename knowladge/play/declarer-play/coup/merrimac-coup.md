# Merrimac Coup Technique

## Overview

The **Merrimac Coup** is an advanced declarer-play technique in which declarer deliberately **sacrifices a high card to force an opponent's vital entry to win a trick prematurely**. By doing so, declarer removes the defender's only or best entry to partner's established winners, preventing the defense from cashing additional tricks later.

The coup is named after the American Civil War ironclad **CSS Merrimac (Virginia)**, which deliberately rammed an opposing ship to disable it. In bridge, declarer similarly "rams" one defensive honor into another to destroy the defenders' communication.

The Merrimac Coup is primarily used in **Notrump contracts**, although it occasionally arises in suit contracts.

---

# Purpose

The Merrimac Coup allows declarer to:

- Eliminate a dangerous defensive entry.
- Break communication between the defenders.
- Isolate an opponent's long suit.
- Prevent defenders from cashing established winners.
- Improve the chances of making the contract.

---

# Basic Principle

The guiding principle is:

> **Sacrifice a winner to destroy a more dangerous entry.**

Declarer intentionally loses a valuable card to a defender whose entry must be removed before that defender can later reach partner's long suit.

---

# When to Use

A Merrimac Coup is appropriate when:

- One defender has an important outside entry.
- The other defender has an established long suit.
- Removing the entry strands those winners.
- The sacrificed trick is affordable.
- Communication between the defenders is the main danger.

---

# Requirements

A successful Merrimac Coup usually requires:

- A dangerous defensive communication.
- An expendable high card.
- Accurate counting.
- Proper timing.
- Recognition of the critical entry.

---

# Relationship to Other Techniques

The Merrimac Coup is closely related to:

- Hold-Up Play
- Scissors Coup
- Avoidance Play
- Entry Management
- Discovery Play

Unlike a **Hold-Up Play**, which usually cuts communication by ducking, the Merrimac Coup destroys communication by **forcing a defender's entry to be used immediately**.

---

# Example 1 – Classic Merrimac Coup

## Contract

```
3NT
```

West has established:

```
♠ KQJ10
```

East holds:

```
♠ A
```

The ♠A is East's only entry to West's long spades.

Declarer leads the ♠K from hand into East's Ace.

East is forced to win immediately.

Later, when West gains the lead, East no longer has the ♠A to regain the lead and enjoy West's established spades.

The defensive communication has been destroyed.

---

# Example 2 – Sacrificing an Honor

Dummy

```
♦ AQJ
```

Declarer

```
♦ K54
```

East's ♦A is the only entry to West's long clubs.

Declarer deliberately drives out the ♦A before establishing clubs.

Although a diamond trick is lost, West's club winners later become unreachable.

---

# Example 3 – Entry Removal

West has long hearts.

East's ♥A is West's only outside entry.

Declarer forces East to win the ♥A early.

Later, West cannot reach East, and the long hearts remain stranded.

---

# Planning the Play

Before Trick One, ask:

- Which defender has the dangerous long suit?
- Which defender holds the critical entry?
- Can I force that entry to win now?
- Is the sacrificed trick affordable?
- Will communication be broken afterward?

---

# Timing

Timing is essential.

Typical sequence:

1. Identify the dangerous entry.
2. Sacrifice the appropriate honor.
3. Force the entry to be used.
4. Establish your winners.
5. Cash them before the defenders reconnect.

---

# Common Situations

The Merrimac Coup frequently occurs:

- In Notrump contracts.
- Against established long suits.
- When one defender has a single outside entry.
- Before establishing a long suit.
- In conjunction with Hold-Up Play or Avoidance Play.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Sacrificing the wrong honor.
- Misidentifying the critical entry.
- Destroying your own communication.
- Waiting until the defenders have already used their winners.
- Miscounting entries.

---

# Defensive Countermeasures

Defenders should:

- Preserve important entries.
- Avoid exposing which hand holds the critical communication.
- Cash long-suit winners before communication is threatened.
- Recognize declarer's attempt to remove an entry.
- Maintain multiple entries whenever possible.

---

# Advantages

- Eliminates vital defensive communication.
- Neutralizes established long suits.
- Prevents defenders from cashing winners.
- Often transforms a losing contract into a making one.
- Powerful against Notrump defenses.

---

# Disadvantages

- Requires sacrificing a trick.
- Depends on accurate counting.
- Timing is critical.
- Ineffective if defenders have multiple entries.

# Memory Aid

**Merrimac Coup = "Sink the Entry Before It Sinks You."**

Remember:

- **The target is the defenders' communication, not an immediate trick.**
- **Be willing to sacrifice a high card if it destroys a vital entry.**
- **The coup is most effective in Notrump contracts.**
- **Count the defenders' entries before committing.**
- **A single removed entry can strand an entire long suit.**

The **Merrimac Coup** is one of bridge's classic communication plays. By intentionally sacrificing a high card to eliminate a defender's key entry, declarer can isolate the defenders' long suit and prevent them from cashing established winners. Success depends on accurate counting, careful timing, and recognizing which defensive entry is truly critical.
