# Eight Ever, Nine Never

## Overview

**"Eight Ever, Nine Never"** is one of the best-known bridge maxims. It provides a guideline for deciding whether to **finesse** or **play for the drop** when missing the queen in a suit.

The saying summarizes the odds when **no additional information** is available:

- **With eight cards in the suit, finesse.**
- **With nine cards in the suit, play for the drop.**

Although easy to remember, it is **only a starting point**. Information from the bidding, card play, Restricted Choice, Vacant Spaces, and counting often outweighs the basic percentages.

---

# The Meaning

Suppose your partnership holds all but one honor:

```
A K J x opposite x x x
```

You are missing only the **Queen**.

You must decide between:

- **Finesse** against one opponent.
- **Play for the Queen to drop** under the Ace and King.

The correct choice depends primarily on how many cards your side holds in the suit.

---

# The Rule

| Cards Held | Normal Percentage Play |
|------------|------------------------|
| 8 cards | Finesse ("Ever") |
| 9 cards | Play for the Drop ("Never") |

Hence the mnemonic:

> **Eight Ever, Nine Never.**

---

# Why Does It Work?

The rule is based on the most likely distributions of the missing cards.

## Eight-Card Fit

Example:

```
Dummy
A J 8 4

Declarer
K 7 6 3
```

Missing:

```
Q 10 9 5 2
```

The finesse succeeds whenever the queen is onside.

Its probability is approximately **50%**, making it superior to relying on the queen falling doubleton.

---

## Nine-Card Fit

Example:

```
Dummy
A J 8 5

Declarer
K 7 6 4 3
```

Missing:

```
Q 10 9 2
```

Now the chance that the queen falls in the normal distributions is slightly higher than the chance of a successful finesse, making **playing for the drop** the percentage action.

---

# The Actual Percentages

Approximate odds with **no additional information**:

| Situation | Best Play | Approximate Chance |
|-----------|-----------|-------------------:|
| Eight-card fit | Finesse | 50% |
| Nine-card fit | Play for drop | 52% |

The difference is modest, especially with nine cards.

---

# When the Rule Does NOT Apply

The maxim assumes:

- No information from the auction.
- No clues from defenders' play.
- No inference from distribution.
- No Restricted Choice.
- No Vacant Spaces analysis.

Whenever additional information exists, it should usually take precedence.

---

# Restricted Choice

Suppose:

```
West follows with the Queen.
```

Restricted Choice changes the probabilities.

Instead of automatically following "Nine Never," the principle may indicate that the remaining honor is more likely to be singleton, making the finesse or alternate line more attractive.

See:

- Restricted Choice

---

# Vacant Spaces

Suppose you know:

- West started with six spades.
- East started with only two.

East now has significantly more **vacant spaces**.

The missing queen is therefore more likely to be with East, and the finesse may become correct even in a nine-card fit.

---

# Information from the Auction

Bidding often changes the odds.

Example:

West opened:

```
1♠
```

West is more likely to hold additional spades.

If deciding whether to finesse in spades, this information may outweigh the basic "Eight Ever, Nine Never" guideline.

---

# Information from Card Play

As the hand develops, defenders reveal:

- Distribution
- Count
- High-card strength
- Suit preference

These inferences frequently produce a more accurate decision than relying solely on the mnemonic.

---

# Example 1

```
Dummy
♠ A J 8 4

Declarer
♠ K 7 6 3
```

Missing:

```
Q 10 9 5 2
```

With no further information:

Take the finesse.

---

# Example 2

```
Dummy
♥ A J 8 5

Declarer
♥ K 7 6 4
```

Missing:

```
Q 10 9 3
```

With no clues:

Cash the Ace and King.

---

# Example 3

During the play you discover:

- West has shown six clubs.
- East has shown only two clubs.

East has more vacant spaces.

If the missing queen is in clubs, the finesse toward East may now become the superior percentage play.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Treating the rule as absolute.
- Ignoring information from the auction.
- Ignoring Restricted Choice.
- Ignoring Vacant Spaces.
- Forgetting to count defenders' distributions.
- Applying the maxim after significant information has been revealed.

# Key Principles

- "Eight Ever, Nine Never" is a **guideline**, not a law.
- With an eight-card fit, the finesse is usually the percentage play.
- With a nine-card fit, playing for the drop is usually slightly better.
- Additional information from the auction or play is often more valuable than the basic percentages.
- Expert players use counting, Restricted Choice, and Vacant Spaces to refine the decision rather than relying solely on the mnemonic.
