# Opening Leads from AK10

## Overview

The holding **Ace-King-Ten (AK10)** is a **broken honor combination** that requires careful judgment when choosing the opening lead. Unlike **AKQ** or **AKJ**, the missing Queen and Jack make it less certain that the suit can be established immediately.

In most modern partnerships, **AK10 is treated the same as AK** for opening-lead purposes:

- Lead the **Ace** to obtain information from partner.
- Use partner's signal (count or attitude) to decide whether to continue.
- Avoid automatically cashing the King unless the information indicates it is correct.

The guiding principle is:

> **Lead for information first, then decide whether continuing the suit will benefit the defense.**

---

# Purpose

Opening leads from AK10 aim to:

- Cash immediate winners.
- Discover partner's distribution.
- Determine whether the Ten can be promoted.
- Look for ruff opportunities in suit contracts.
- Prevent declarer from establishing side suits.

---

# Understanding the Holding

Example:

```
♠ A K 10 7 4
```

Strengths:

- Two immediate winners.
- Potential to establish additional tricks if partner has support.

Weaknesses:

- Missing both the Queen and Jack.
- Continuing blindly may help declarer establish the suit.

---

# Standard Expert Treatment

| Contract | Typical Lead | Purpose |
|----------|--------------|----------|
| Notrump | Ace | Ask for attitude or count |
| Suit Contract | Ace | Obtain count and evaluate a possible ruff |
| Special Agreements | King (rare) | Only if specifically agreed |

Most expert partnerships treat **AK10 exactly like AK**.

---

# Against Notrump

## Objectives

- Establish the long suit.
- Learn whether partner has the Queen or Jack.
- Decide whether to continue or switch.

### Example

Holding

```
♣ A K 10 8 5
```

Contract

```
3NT
```

Lead:

```
♣A
```

Partner's signal:

- Encourage with useful honors or length.
- Discourage with poor support.

Continue only if the signal and the auction justify it.

---

# Against Suit Contracts

## Objectives

- Cash immediate winners.
- Determine whether partner has a doubleton.
- Look for a ruff.
- Avoid giving declarer unnecessary tempo.

Example

Holding

```
♥ A K 10 6
```

Contract

```
4♠
```

Lead:

```
♥A
```

Partner gives count.

If partner shows a doubleton, continue hearts to obtain a ruff.

---

# Partner's Signals

## Count

Many partnerships request **count** after an Ace lead.

### Standard Count

```
High-Low = Even

Low-High = Odd
```

### UDCA

```
High-Low = Odd

Low-High = Even
```

---

## Attitude

Other partnerships prefer:

- High = Encourage.
- Low = Discourage.

This helps determine whether continuing the suit is worthwhile.

---

## Suit Preference

After count has been established, Suit Preference may help identify the best switch or return.

---

# Example 1 – Partner Has the Queen

Holding

```
♦ A K 10 9
```

Partner encourages.

Later the Queen appears from partner.

The Ten becomes a valuable winner.

---

# Example 2 – Partner Shows Doubleton

Holding

```
♠ A K 10 5
```

Contract

```
4♥
```

Lead:

```
♠A
```

Partner gives count indicating two spades.

Continue the suit.

Partner scores a ruff.

---

# Example 3 – Switch After the Ace

Holding

```
♣ A K 10 4
```

Partner discourages.

Instead of automatically cashing the King, switch to another suit.

Preserving the King may later improve communication or avoid helping declarer.

---

# Planning the Defense

Before leading AK10, ask:

- Do I need count or attitude?
- Am I trying to establish the Ten?
- Is a ruff possible?
- Should I continue after Trick One?
- Does the auction suggest another opening lead?

---

# Common Mistakes

- Treating AK10 like AKQ.
- Automatically cashing both top honors.
- Ignoring partner's signal.
- Continuing after a discouraging signal.
- Forgetting the auction.

---

# Partnership Agreements

Before play, discuss:

- Ace or King from AK10?
- Does the Ace request count or attitude?
- Different methods in Notrump and suit contracts.
- When Suit Preference applies.
- Whether AK10 is treated exactly like AK.

---

# Advantages

- Two immediate winners.
- Flexible continuation after partner's signal.
- May establish the Ten.
- Creates opportunities for ruffs.
- Fits naturally into standard Ace-lead methods.

---

# Disadvantages

- Missing both intermediate honors may limit the suit's potential.
- Continuing automatically may help declarer.
- Requires careful partnership understanding.
- Timing is important.

# Memory Guide

## Five Steps

1. Lead the agreed honor.
2. Read partner's signal.
3. Evaluate whether the suit should continue.
4. Look for ruff opportunities.
5. Preserve communication if switching is better.

# Memory Aid

**AK10 Leads = "Information First, Continuation Second."**

Remember:

- **AK10 is a broken honor combination, not a solid sequence.**
- **Most expert partnerships treat AK10 exactly like AK and lead the Ace.**
- **Do not cash the King automatically—partner's signal should guide the continuation.**
- **Against suit contracts, always consider whether partner may be able to score a ruff.**
- **Good judgment and partnership agreements are more important than the holding itself.**

Opening leads from **AK10** require patience and accurate partnership communication. Although the holding contains two immediate winners, the missing Queen and Jack mean that continuing the suit is not always correct. By gathering information first and evaluating the defense carefully, expert partnerships maximize the value of this strong but imperfect honor combination.
