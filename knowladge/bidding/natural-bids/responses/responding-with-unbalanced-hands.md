# Responding with Unbalanced Hands

## Bridge Insight

**Unbalanced hands derive much of their strength from distribution rather than High Card Points. Long suits, short suits, and trump support often create tricks that cannot be measured by HCP alone.**

---

# Overview

An **unbalanced hand** contains unusual distribution, such as a singleton, void, six-card suit, or two long suits. Unlike balanced hands, whose value is determined primarily by High Card Points, unbalanced hands frequently gain strength from their ability to establish long suits, create ruffs, and generate extra tricks.

When responding with an unbalanced hand, responder's objective is to describe both **strength** and **shape**, allowing opener to evaluate the partnership's combined playing potential.

Although bidding systems differ in the meanings of specific responses, the fundamental principles of bidding unbalanced hands are common to virtually all natural systems.

---

# Objectives

Responder should attempt to:

- Describe distribution accurately.
- Show long suits.
- Locate a trump fit.
- Reveal shortages when appropriate.
- Maximize the partnership's trick-taking potential.
- Leave room for accurate future bidding.

---

# What Is an Unbalanced Hand?

Typical unbalanced distributions include:

```
6-3-2-2

5-4-2-2

5-4-3-1

6-4-2-1

5-5-2-1

7-2-2-2
```

More generally, an unbalanced hand contains at least one of:

- Singleton
- Void
- Six-card (or longer) suit
- Two long suits

---

# Evaluating an Unbalanced Hand

Responder should evaluate:

- High Card Points
- Distribution
- Suit quality
- Controls
- Playing strength
- Trump support

Distribution often adds significant value beyond HCP.

---

# Long Suits

Long suits provide:

- Extra tricks
- Establishment potential
- Safe entries
- Offensive strength

Responder should normally introduce a good long suit as early as practical.

---

# Short Suits

Singletons and voids are valuable because they:

- Create ruffing opportunities.
- Increase offensive potential.
- Improve slam prospects.

Their value increases dramatically after a trump fit has been found.

---

# Two-Suited Hands

Hands containing two long suits often have exceptional playing strength.

Responder should usually:

- Introduce the longer suit first.
- Describe the second suit later.
- Avoid concealing significant distribution.

---

# Re-evaluating After Finding a Fit

Once opener supports responder's suit—or responder supports opener's suit—the value of the hand changes.

Responder should increase the value of:

- Shortages
- Extra trump length
- Controls
- Ruffing potential

Flat High Card Point evaluation is no longer sufficient.

---

# Distribution versus High Card Points

Consider:

Hand A

```
♠ K Q 8
♥ A J 7
♦ Q 8 5
♣ J 7 3
```

13 HCP

Balanced.

---

Hand B

```
♠ A Q J 10 7
♥ K J 9 8 6
♦ 3
♣ 7 2
```

Similar HCP.

Much greater playing strength.

Distribution frequently produces additional tricks.

---

# Major Suit Priorities

When holding an unbalanced hand:

- Show four-card or longer majors.
- Search for an eight-card fit.
- Consider ruffing potential.
- Avoid committing to Notrump too early.

---

# Minor Suit Hands

Long minors often require patience.

Responder may:

- Introduce the minor naturally.
- Show another suit first.
- Delay support until more information is available.

The correct approach depends upon the auction.

---

# Planning the Auction

Responder should think beyond the first response.

Questions include:

- Can I describe both suits?
- Will partner understand my distribution?
- Should I force?
- Is game realistic?
- Could this become a slam auction?

---

# Offensive versus Defensive Strength

Unbalanced hands are usually:

- Better offensively.
- Worse defensively.

Responder should therefore emphasize:

- Playing strength.
- Controls.
- Fit.

rather than relying only on HCP.

---

# System Variations

Natural systems differ mainly in:

- Strength requirements.
- Forcing nature of responses.
- Treatment of two-suited hands.

The underlying evaluation principles remain essentially the same.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Valuing the hand only by HCP.
- Concealing a long suit.
- Ignoring a singleton after finding a fit.
- Bidding Notrump too early.
- Underestimating distributional strength.

---

# Best Practices

Experienced responders:

- Re-evaluate after every new piece of information.
- Think in tricks rather than points.
- Describe shape honestly.
- Look for offensive contracts.
- Preserve bidding space whenever possible.

# Player's Checklist

Before responding with an unbalanced hand ask:

- What is my longest suit?
- Do I have a second suit?
- Do I have a singleton or void?
- Have I found a fit?
- Should I revalue my hand?
- Am I bidding points or playing strength?

---

# Memory Guide

## Six Principles

1. Count points.
2. Count shape.
3. Show your longest suit.
4. Find the trump fit.
5. Re-evaluate after support.
6. Think in tricks.

---

# Expert Tip

**Distribution wins contracts.**

Many expert game and slam bids are based more on shape than on High Card Points. Learn to recognize when your distribution creates trick-taking power that the point count cannot measure.

---

# Memory Aid

**Unbalanced Hands = Shape Creates Strength**

Remember:

- Long suits establish winners.
- Short suits create ruffs.
- Distribution increases offensive potential.
- Re-evaluate after a fit is discovered.
- Describe your shape honestly and let partner judge the combined strength.

An unbalanced hand is often far stronger than its High Card Point count suggests. Successful responders recognize that distribution, controls, and trump support are the true sources of offensive power, and they bid in a way that allows the partnership to discover and exploit those strengths.
