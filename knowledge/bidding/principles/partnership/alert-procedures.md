# Alert Procedures

## Overview

**Alert Procedures** are used in duplicate bridge to ensure that both opponents are fully informed about partnership agreements that may not have an obvious or natural meaning. The purpose of an alert is **not to help partner**, but to provide the opponents with the same understanding of the partnership's agreements.

An alert tells the opponents:

> **"This bid has a special or artificial meaning. You are entitled to know what our agreement is."**

The exact alert regulations vary slightly among bridge organizations such as the **World Bridge Federation (WBF)**, **American Contract Bridge League (ACBL)**, and **European Bridge League (EBL)**. Players should always follow the regulations governing the event.

The guiding principle is:

> **Opponents are entitled to your agreements—not your conclusions.**

---

# Purpose

Alert procedures help to:

- Promote fair competition.
- Ensure full disclosure.
- Prevent misunderstandings.
- Inform opponents of artificial meanings.
- Maintain ethical standards.

---

# What Is an Alert?

An **Alert** is a notification that a bid has a meaning the opponents would not normally expect.

The alert does **not** explain the bid by itself.

It simply tells the opponents that they may ask for an explanation.

---

# What Must Be Disclosed?

You must disclose:

- Partnership agreements.
- Conventional meanings.
- Artificial bids.
- Special treatments.
- Unusual responses.
- Defensive methods.

You **do not** disclose:

- Personal opinions.
- Hand evaluations.
- Guesses.
- Inferences.

---

# Natural vs. Artificial Bids

## Natural Bid

Example:

```
1♠

Shows spades.
```

Normally no alert is required (subject to local regulations).

---

## Artificial Bid

Example:

```
2♣

Strong artificial opening.
```

This usually requires an alert.

---

# Commonly Alerted Bids

Depending on local regulations, examples include:

- Stayman
- Transfers
- Jacoby 2NT
- Bergen Raises
- Splinters
- Michaels Cue Bid
- Unusual Notrump
- Cappelletti
- DONT
- Precision openings
- Artificial responses
- Relay bids
- Puppet Stayman

Always consult the regulations for your governing organization.

---

# Explaining a Bid

When an opponent asks:

```
"What does that bid mean?"
```

Answer with:

- Your partnership agreement.
- The agreed range.
- The agreed distribution.
- The agreed forcing status.

Example:

```
"2♦ shows a transfer to hearts."
```

Do **not** say:

```
"I think partner has five hearts."
```

Explain the agreement, not your interpretation of the current hand.

---

# If There Is No Agreement

If your partnership has no agreement:

Say exactly that.

Example:

```
"We have no partnership agreement."

or

"We have not discussed this situation."
```

Never invent an explanation.

---

# Self-Alerts

Under WBF regulations, many calls made by the declaring side in online bridge or certain special situations may be **self-alerted**, meaning the player explains their own bid rather than partner alerting it. Procedures differ by organization and by whether play is online or face-to-face.

Always follow the event's regulations.

---

# Announcement vs. Alert

Some organizations distinguish between:

## Announcement

A short prescribed statement made immediately after certain bids.

Examples (depending on regulations):

```
"Transfer."

"15–17."

"Could be short."
```

---

## Alert

A notification indicating that opponents may ask for an explanation.

The exact distinction depends on the governing body.

---

# Ethical Responsibilities

Players must:

- Disclose agreements fully.
- Explain honestly.
- Avoid misleading statements.
- Correct mistaken explanations when required by the Laws.
- Never use alerts to communicate with partner.

---

# Convention Cards

Your convention card should accurately record:

- Opening bids.
- Responses.
- Conventions.
- Lead agreements.
- Defensive signals.
- Competitive methods.

The card supports proper disclosure.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Explaining the current hand instead of the agreement.
- Forgetting to alert artificial bids.
- Giving incomplete explanations.
- Using private partnership understandings not recorded on the convention card.
- Assuming opponents already know the convention.

# Advantages

Proper alert procedures:

- Promote fairness.
- Reduce misunderstandings.
- Improve tournament ethics.
- Protect both partnerships.
- Encourage accurate disclosure.

---

# Limitations

Alert procedures:

- Do not replace convention cards.
- Do not reveal hand strength beyond the partnership agreement.
- Differ among bridge organizations.
- Require players to stay familiar with current regulations.

# Player's Checklist

Before every session:

- Review partnership agreements.
- Complete the convention card.
- Know which bids require alerts or announcements.
- Explain agreements—not conclusions.
- Follow the event's regulations.

---

# Memory Guide

## Five Rules

1. Alert special agreements.
2. Explain partnership agreements only.
3. Never explain your current hand.
4. Be honest and complete.
5. Follow the regulations of the sponsoring organization.

---

# Typical Alert Process

```
Partner Makes a Bid
↓

Is It Alertable?

↓

Yes

↓

Alert (or Announce, if Required)

↓

Opponent Asks

↓

Explain the Partnership Agreement

↓

Continue the Auction
```

---

# Memory Aid

**Alert Procedures = "Disclose Agreements, Not Thoughts."**

Remember:

- **An alert informs the opponents that a bid has a special agreed meaning.**
- **Always explain your partnership agreement, never your interpretation of partner's hand.**
- **If there is no agreement, say so honestly.**
- **Alert and announcement requirements vary by governing organization and event.**
- **Fair disclosure is a cornerstone of duplicate bridge ethics and sportsmanship.**

Alert procedures are an essential part of duplicate bridge. They ensure that both partnerships compete with equal access to partnership agreements, preserving fairness and integrity. Accurate alerts, complete explanations, and honest disclosure are hallmarks of ethical and successful bridge partnerships.
