# Competitive Bidding Philosophy

## Overview

**Competitive Bidding** occurs whenever both partnerships compete for the final contract. Success depends not only on the strength of your own hand, but also on accurately judging:

- The total strength of both sides.
- The likely final contract.
- Vulnerability.
- The scoring method (Matchpoints or IMPs).
- Distribution.
- Partnership agreements.

Expert players recognize that competitive bidding is **a battle for the best score**, not necessarily for the contract itself.

The guiding principle is:

> **Compete when the expected gain exceeds the expected risk.**

---

# Purpose

Competitive bidding aims to:

- Win the contract.
- Push opponents to an uncomfortable level.
- Find the best strain.
- Prevent opponents from exchanging information.
- Discover profitable sacrifices.
- Maximize the final score.

---

# The Competitive Mindset

When the opponents enter the auction, ask:

- Which side owns the hand?
- Do we have the majority of points?
- Do we have the better fit?
- Is defending more profitable?
- Should we compete, sacrifice, or double?

Every decision should balance **reward against risk**.

---

# Fundamental Principles

## 1. Distribution Is Powerful

Shape often outweighs High Card Points.

Hands with:

- Long suits
- Shortages
- Two-suited distribution

are frequently worth competing with even when HCP are modest.

Example:

```
♠ K Q J 10 8 6
♥ 7
♦ J 8 4
♣ Q 10 5
```

The six-card suit makes this hand much stronger offensively than its HCP alone suggest.

---

## 2. Fit Is More Important Than Points

An eight-card fit frequently justifies competition.

A nine-card fit often justifies bidding one level higher.

Finding a fit is usually the first priority.

---

## 3. The Law of Total Tricks

The **Law of Total Tricks (LoTT)** suggests that the total number of tricks available to both sides is approximately equal to the total number of trumps held by both partnerships.

Example:

```
We have 9 trumps.

Opponents have 8 trumps.

Total ≈17 tricks.
```

This principle often guides competitive decisions but should not be followed blindly.

---

## 4. Vulnerability Changes Everything

Not Vulnerable:

- Compete more aggressively.
- Sacrifices become more attractive.
- Light overcalls are more acceptable.

Vulnerable:

- Compete more carefully.
- Respect penalty doubles.
- Avoid speculative sacrifices.

---

## 5. Matchpoints vs. IMPs

### Matchpoints

Encourages:

- Active competition.
- Fighting for part-scores.
- Thin overcalls.
- Every extra 10 or 20 points.

### IMPs

Encourages:

- Sound judgment.
- Avoiding expensive penalties.
- Conservative sacrifices.
- Making contracts rather than winning small battles.

---

# Offensive vs. Defensive Values

Ask whether your hand is better suited to:

- Declaring.
- Defending.

Indicators of offensive strength:

- Long suits.
- Shortages.
- Controls.
- Fit.

Indicators of defensive strength:

- Balanced hands.
- High honors in opponents' suits.
- Quick tricks.
- Few distributional values.

---

# Competing with Shape

Example:

```
♠ K Q J 10 8 4
♥ 6
♦ 9 4
♣ A J 8 3
```

Even with limited HCP, the long spade suit justifies entering the auction.

Distribution creates tricks.

---

# Competitive Decisions

## Compete

Choose to compete when:

- You have a fit.
- Distribution favors offense.
- Vulnerability is favorable.
- The expected gain exceeds the risk.

---

## Defend

Choose to defend when:

- Opponents clearly own the hand.
- Your hand contains defensive values.
- Competing risks a large penalty.

---

## Sacrifice

A sacrifice is correct when:

```
Penalty

<

Opponent's likely score.
```

Example:

```
Opponents:

620

Your sacrifice:

-300

Good sacrifice.
```

---

## Double

Penalty doubles should be based on:

- Defensive strength.
- Limited offensive potential.
- Accurate trump expectations.
- Favorable vulnerability.

---

# Communication

Competitive auctions provide information.

Pay attention to:

- Opponents' suit lengths.
- Strength ranges.
- Fits.
- Distribution.

Every bid changes the picture.

---

# Planning the Auction

Before every competitive bid, ask:

- Who owns the hand?
- Who has the fit?
- What is the vulnerability?
- What is the scoring method?
- Is offense or defense more profitable?

---

# Common Mistakes

- Competing without a fit.
- Ignoring vulnerability.
- Sacrificing while vulnerable.
- Overvaluing High Card Points.
- Ignoring distribution.
- Using Matchpoint strategy at IMPs.

---

# Advantages

Good competitive bidding:

- Wins more part-score battles.
- Finds profitable sacrifices.
- Pushes opponents into mistakes.
- Maximizes long-term scoring.
- Improves partnership judgment.

# Comparison

|-----------|--------------------|
| Long suit | Compete more aggressively |
| Good fit | Raise the level of competition |
| Defensive hand | Consider defending or doubling |
| Favorable vulnerability | Accept more risk |
| Unfavorable vulnerability | Bid conservatively |

```
Evaluate Your Hand
↓

↓

Consider Vulnerability

↓

Consider Matchpoints or IMPs

↓

Compare Offense vs. Defense

↓

Choose to Bid, Double, Sacrifice, or Defend
```

---

# Memory Aid

**Competitive Bidding = "Compete with Purpose, Not Hope."**

Remember:

- **Distribution and fit are often more important than High Card Points.**
- **Always compare the value of declaring with the value of defending.**
- **Vulnerability and the scoring method should influence every competitive decision.**
- **A profitable sacrifice is often better than allowing opponents to make game.**
- **Expert competitive bidding is about making the decision with the highest expected value, not simply winning the auction.**

Competitive bidding is one of the most strategic aspects of bridge. The strongest players understand that every competitive decision is an exercise in judgment—balancing offense against defense, reward against risk, and probability against scoring. Mastering these principles allows partnerships to compete effectively, avoid costly mistakes, and consistently achieve better tournament results.
