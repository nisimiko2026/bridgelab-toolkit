# Lead Partner's Suit

## Overview

One of the oldest and most reliable principles in bridge is:

> **When in doubt, lead partner's suit.**

If partner has bid a suit during the auction, they have already provided valuable information about their hand. Leading that suit is often the safest and most effective way to establish defensive tricks, find partner's honors, or develop a successful defense.

While this principle has many exceptions, it remains the default choice whenever no clearly superior alternative exists.

---

# Why Lead Partner's Suit?

Partner has already indicated:

- Length in the suit.
- At least some strength or playing potential.
- A likely source of defensive tricks.
- A suit they would often prefer you to attack.

Compared with choosing an unbid suit, leading partner's suit relies on information rather than guesswork.

---

# Typical Situations

Leading partner's suit is particularly attractive when partner has:

- Opened the bidding.
- Made an overcall.
- Preempted.
- Rebid the suit.
- Doubled and later identified a preferred suit.

The stronger and more specific partner's bidding, the stronger the case for leading that suit.

---

# Partner's Overcall

Example:

```
West   North   East   South
1♥     1♠      Pass   4♥
All Pass
```

Partner has shown a genuine spade suit.

A spade lead is usually the best opening lead.

---

# Partner's Opening Bid

Example:

```
West   North   East   South
Pass   1♦      Pass   3NT
All Pass
```

Leading a diamond is often correct because partner has shown length and strength.

---

# Partner Has Bid the Suit Twice

When partner repeats a suit, the argument for leading it becomes even stronger.

Example:

```
West   North   East   South
1♣     Pass    1♥     Pass
2♣     Pass    3NT
```

Partner has shown extra club length.

A club lead is frequently the percentage action.

---

# After a Preempt

Partner's preempt usually promises:

- A long suit.
- Good suit quality.
- Limited outside values.

Example:

```
West   North   East   South
3♠     Pass    4♥     All Pass
```

Leading a spade is often correct, especially if declarer has not shown control of the suit.

---

# Honor Leads

When leading partner's suit, continue to use your normal lead agreements.

Examples:

| Holding | Typical Lead |
|---------|--------------|
| K Q J 4 | K |
| Q J 10 5 | Q |
| A J 8 5 | Fourth-best (or agreed method) |
| 9 7 4 | According to partnership agreement (e.g., Top of Nothing or low card) |

Leading partner's suit does **not** change your opening lead methods.

---

# Against Notrump

Leading partner's suit is often the best strategy because:

- Long suits produce established winners.
- Declarer cannot ruff.
- Partner is more likely to have entries.

The defense frequently succeeds by establishing partner's long suit before declarer establishes theirs.

---

# Against Suit Contracts

Leading partner's suit is still a strong default, but additional factors apply:

- Dummy may ruff.
- A trump lead may be superior.
- Declarer may have shown shortness.
- Partner's suit may no longer be safe.

Always consider the auction before making an automatic lead.

---

# Against Slam Contracts

Partner's suit remains a leading candidate, especially when:

- Partner has overcalled naturally.
- The auction suggests partner holds the setting trick.
- The lead may establish an immediate winner.

However, avoid speculative honor leads that may give declarer a free trick.

---

# When Not to Lead Partner's Suit

Leading partner's suit is not automatic.

Consider another lead when:

- Declarer has shown a strong fit in that suit.
- The auction suggests partner's suit is no longer productive.
- A trump lead is clearly indicated.
- A singleton lead offers a likely ruff.
- The contract is a slam and a passive lead is safer.

The auction always takes precedence over general rules.

---

# Reading the Auction

Before leading partner's suit, ask:

- Was partner's bid natural?
- Did partner overcall with minimum values?
- Has declarer cue-bid the suit?
- Has dummy shown support?
- Is partner's suit likely to provide immediate tricks?

The more information available, the more accurate the lead.

---

# Practical Example 1

Auction:

```
West   North   East   South
1♥     Pass    2♥     Pass
4♥     All Pass
```

West holds:

```
♠ K 9 4
♥ 8 6 2
♦ J 7 5
♣ A Q 10 3
```

With no guidance from partner, a passive club or diamond lead may be preferable to leading away from the king of spades.

---

# Practical Example 2

Auction:

```
West   North   East   South
1♦     1♠      4♥     All Pass
```

West holds:

```
♠ 9 7 4
♥ K 6 2
♦ Q J 8 5
♣ A 10 4
```

A spade lead is the percentage action because partner has shown a natural spade suit.

---

# Practical Example 3

Auction:

```
West   North   East   South
3♣     Pass    3NT    All Pass
```

West holds:

```
♣ K 8 4
♦ 10 7 5
♥ J 9 3
♠ Q 10 6
```

Leading a club is usually correct because partner's preempt promises a long suit that may quickly become established.

---

# Partnership Agreements

Discuss:

- Honor lead style (Standard or Rusinow).
- Fourth-best, Third-and-Fifth, or other spot-card methods.
- Top of Nothing.
- Ace and king lead agreements.
- Interior sequence leads.
- Whether partner's preempt always requests a lead.
- Lead priorities after takeout doubles.

Consistent agreements help partner interpret both the opening lead and subsequent defense.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Automatically leading partner's suit without considering the auction.
- Ignoring a clearly superior trump lead.
- Leading away from an unsupported honor simply because it is partner's suit.
- Failing to use the agreed lead method.
- Forgetting that declarer may have shown strength or shortness in partner's suit.

---

# Checklist Before Leading Partner's Suit

Ask yourself:

1. Has partner naturally shown this suit?
2. Has declarer shown strength or control in the suit?
3. Will the lead help establish immediate tricks?
4. Is a trump lead more attractive?
5. Am I following our agreed lead methods?
6. Is there a safer passive alternative?

# Key Principles

- Leading partner's suit is the standard opening lead when no stronger alternative exists.
- Trust the information partner has provided through the auction.
- Continue to use your normal honor and spot-card lead agreements.
- Do not lead partner's suit automatically—always consider the auction and the contract.
- Let the combination of the bidding and your partnership agreements guide the final decision.
