# Discard Strategy

## Overview

A **discard strategy** is the defensive plan for choosing which cards to throw away when unable to follow suit. Every discard provides an opportunity to:

- communicate with partner,
- preserve important holdings,
- mislead declarer,
- or prepare for the endgame.

Expert defenders recognize that a discard is **never "just a discard."** Every card played conveys information or affects the future play of the hand.

A good discard balances **communication, deception, and suit preservation**.

---

# Objectives

An effective discard strategy aims to:

- Communicate useful information to partner.
- Preserve potential winners and entries.
- Keep safe exit cards.
- Avoid helping declarer count the hand.
- Prepare for the likely endgame.

---

# Basic Principle

Before discarding, ask:

1. Which suit is least valuable?
2. What information does partner need?
3. What information should declarer *not* receive?
4. Will I need this card as an entry or exit later?

A discard should always support the overall defensive plan.

---

# Establish Defensive Priorities

Before selecting a discard, identify:

- defensive winners,
- possible entries,
- partner's established suits,
- declarer's likely source of tricks,
- dangerous suits.

Only then decide which cards can safely be released.

---

# Choosing a Suit to Discard

In general, discard from:

- hopeless suits,
- suits already controlled by declarer,
- long weak suits with no future value,
- exhausted suits.

Avoid discarding from:

- partner's known suit,
- guarded honors,
- potential entries,
- safe exit cards,
- suits needed for communication.

---

# Discarding from Honor Holdings

Be cautious when discarding from:

```
Kx
Qx
Jx
KJx
QJx
```

A single discard may:

- unguard an honor,
- establish declarer's winner,
- remove a stopper,
- create an endplay.

Always consider the long-term consequences.

---

# Defensive Signals Through Discards

Depending on partnership agreements, discards may show:

## Attitude

Encourage or discourage continuation of a suit.

Example:

- High card = encourage.
- Low card = discourage.

(Reverse if using upside-down methods.)

---

## Count

Reveal an even or odd number of cards in a suit.

Useful when partner needs to know declarer's distribution.

---

## Suit Preference

Indicate interest in one of the remaining suits.

Typically:

- High discard = higher-ranking side suit.
- Low discard = lower-ranking side suit.

Suit preference is especially valuable after partner has already established a winner.

---

# Preserving Communication

A discard should not destroy communication.

Keep cards that:

- provide entries,
- unblock partner's suit when necessary,
- maintain flexibility,
- preserve future switches.

Many contracts succeed because defenders discard essential communication cards.

---

# Planning for the Endgame

Early discards often determine the final position.

Before discarding, ask:

- Could this become my only safe exit?
- Might I need this stopper later?
- Could declarer execute a throw-in?
- Will this guard an important suit?

Thinking several tricks ahead prevents many endplay disasters.

---

# Discarding Against Notrump Contracts

Priorities include:

- preserving stoppers,
- keeping partner's long suit alive,
- maintaining entries,
- communicating count,
- avoiding unnecessary reveals.

Discarding a stopper too early may concede the contract.

---

# Discarding Against Trump Contracts

In suit contracts, consider:

- preserving trump control,
- keeping ruffing opportunities,
- maintaining safe exits,
- guarding side-suit honors,
- preventing trump promotion.

The best discard often differs from the obvious discard.

---

# Falsecarding

Occasionally defenders deliberately discard misleading cards.

Examples include:

- concealing count,
- disguising suit length,
- pretending to encourage another suit,
- hiding an honor.

False discards should be used selectively and only when they are unlikely to confuse partner.

---

# Common Defensive Errors

## Discarding Automatically

Choosing the smallest card without thought often gives away valuable information or destroys communication.

---

## Unguarding Honors

Discarding the last protection for a king or queen frequently gives declarer an extra trick.

---

## Forgetting Partner's Suit

Discarding from partner's established suit may eliminate future defensive winners.

---

## Destroying Safe Exits

Late in the hand, even a small card may be the only harmless return.

---

## Giving Declarer Count

Careless discards may reveal the exact distribution of the defenders' hands.

---

# Practical Questions

Before every discard, ask:

- Which suit no longer matters?
- Does partner need count or attitude?
- Am I preserving my entries?
- Am I protecting important honors?
- Could this card become a safe exit?
- What information will declarer learn?

These questions help ensure every discard supports the defensive plan.

# Practical Tips

- Plan your first discard before it becomes necessary.
- Preserve partner's established suit whenever possible.
- Avoid unguarding honors without a clear reason.
- Use partnership signaling agreements consistently.
- Think about the endgame before releasing low cards.
- Falsecard only when partner is unlikely to be misled.
- Remember that every discard communicates something—intentionally or not.

---

# Key Takeaways

- Every defensive discard should serve a purpose.
- Good discards preserve communication, entries, and important guards.
- Signaling through discards is a fundamental part of partnership defense.
- Poor discards often lose contracts several tricks later.
- Expert defenders treat every discard as an opportunity to improve the defense while revealing as little as possible to declarer.
