# Probability in Bridge

## Overview

Probability is one of the foundations of expert bridge. Every decision—whether to finesse, play for the drop, choose between two suits, or defend a contract—involves estimating the likelihood of different outcomes.

Strong players combine **mathematical probability** with **information gathered during the auction and play**. As more information becomes available, probabilities change, allowing players to make increasingly accurate decisions.

Probability should guide decisions, but it never replaces careful counting, inference, and planning.

---

# Two Types of Probability

Bridge decisions rely on two kinds of probability:

## A Priori Probability

The probability **before** any information has been revealed.

Examples:

- Expected suit distributions.
- Odds of a finesse.
- Percentage plays.
- "Eight Ever, Nine Never."

These probabilities are based solely on the mathematics of the deal.

---

## A Posteriori Probability

The probability **after** information has been obtained from:

- The auction.
- Opening lead.
- Card play.
- Signals.
- Distribution already revealed.

Expert players constantly update their estimates as the hand develops.

---

# Sources of Information

Probability improves as information accumulates.

Useful sources include:

- Bidding.
- Opening lead.
- Carding.
- Revealed suit lengths.
- High cards already played.
- Defensive signals.
- Tempo and timing (where appropriate).

Every revealed card changes the odds.

---

# Percentage Plays

A **percentage play** is the line of play that has the highest probability of success.

Examples:

- Finesse versus playing for the drop.
- Which finesse to take first.
- Which opponent is more likely to hold a missing honor.
- Whether to establish a long suit or draw trumps first.

The highest-percentage play is not always successful, but over many deals it produces the best results.

---

# Counting Distribution

One of the strongest probability tools is counting the defenders' shapes.

Example:

West has shown:

- Five spades.
- Four hearts.

West therefore has only four cards remaining in the minor suits.

Knowing this changes the probability of missing honors and suit breaks.

Counting often outweighs basic percentage tables.

---

# Suit Distribution Probabilities

Certain distributions occur much more frequently than others.

Common examples:

| Missing Cards | Most Likely Split |
|---------------|------------------|
| 2 | 1–1 |
| 3 | 2–1 |
| 4 | 2–2 |
| 5 | 3–2 |
| 6 | 4–2 |
| 7 | 4–3 |
| 8 | 4–4 |

Knowing these frequencies helps declarer choose the best line of play.

---

# Restricted Choice

The **Principle of Restricted Choice** explains how the play of one honor changes the probability of the location of another honor.

Typical applications include:

- Missing Queen-Jack.
- Missing King-Queen.
- Choosing between two finesses.

Restricted Choice is one of the most powerful probability principles in bridge.

---

# Vacant Spaces

The **Vacant Spaces Principle** states that a missing card is more likely to be held by the opponent with more unknown cards remaining.

Example:

West has shown:

- Six spades.
- Three hearts.

East therefore has more vacant spaces.

A missing queen is now more likely to be with East.

---

# Conditional Probability

Bridge probability is rarely static.

Every card played changes the probability of future events.

Examples include:

- Updating the odds after a defender follows with an honor.
- Revising suit-break expectations after defenders show out.
- Reassessing honor locations after the auction.

Expert players continually adjust their expectations as new evidence appears.

---

# Probability vs. Certainty

Probability does **not** predict what will happen on a single deal.

Instead, it identifies the line that succeeds most often over many deals.

For example:

A finesse may have a 50% chance of success.

On any particular hand it either succeeds or fails, but consistently taking the correct percentage action produces the best long-term results.

---

# When Probability Changes

Basic probabilities should be revised whenever new information becomes available.

Examples:

- A defender opens the bidding.
- A defender shows a long suit.
- An honor appears unexpectedly.
- A defender discards from a suit.
- Restricted Choice applies.
- Vacant Spaces favors one defender.

The more you know, the less you rely on raw percentages.

---

# Common Probability Decisions

Players regularly estimate probabilities when deciding whether to:

- Take a finesse.
- Play for the drop.
- Cash winners first.
- Draw trumps immediately.
- Choose between two suits.
- Duck a trick.
- Execute a safety play.
- Decide which defender holds a missing honor.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Ignoring information from the auction.
- Relying solely on memorized percentages.
- Failing to count defenders' distributions.
- Forgetting to update probabilities during play.
- Treating probability as certainty.
- Ignoring Restricted Choice and Vacant Spaces.

# Key Principles

- Probability is a guide, not a guarantee.
- A priori probabilities provide the starting point for decision-making.
- A posteriori probabilities become more accurate as information is revealed.
- Counting and inference usually outweigh memorized percentage tables.
- Expert players continually update probabilities throughout the auction and play.
- The best bridge decisions combine mathematics with observation, planning, and sound judgment.
