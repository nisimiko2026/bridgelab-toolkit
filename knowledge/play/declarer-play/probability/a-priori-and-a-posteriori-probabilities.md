# A Priori and A Posteriori Probabilities

## Overview

Every bridge decision begins with an estimate of probability. Initially, that estimate is based on **a priori probability**—the mathematical likelihood of an event before any information is known.

As the auction and play develop, new information becomes available. The original estimate is refined into an **a posteriori probability**, which incorporates the evidence revealed during the hand.

Expert players constantly transition from a priori thinking to a posteriori thinking, updating their decisions as new facts emerge.

---

# A Priori Probability

An **a priori probability** is the probability of an event **before any relevant information has been obtained**.

Examples include:

- Expected suit distributions.
- Basic finesse odds.
- Probability of a 3–2 trump split.
- Percentage plays.
- "Eight Ever, Nine Never."

These probabilities are derived from mathematics alone.

---

# Examples of A Priori Probability

Before the opening lead:

- A missing queen is equally likely to be with either defender.
- Five missing cards split 3–2 about 68% of the time.
- A finesse succeeds approximately 50% of the time when no additional information exists.

These are all starting assumptions.

---

# A Posteriori Probability

An **a posteriori probability** is the probability of an event **after new information has been observed**.

Sources of information include:

- The auction.
- Opening lead.
- Card play.
- Suit distribution.
- Defender signals.
- Revealed honors.
- Counting.

As evidence accumulates, the original mathematical probabilities become less important than the observed facts.

---

# Example 1 – Before the Auction

You need to locate the:

```
♠Q
```

Initially:

- West: 50%
- East: 50%

This is an **a priori** estimate.

---

# Example 2 – After the Auction

West opens:

```
1♠
```

West has suggested:

- Spade length.
- High-card strength.

The probability that West holds the queen has increased.

This is now an **a posteriori** estimate.

---

# Example 3 – During the Play

West later shows out on the third round of diamonds.

You now know much more about West's shape.

The location of the remaining honors should be reassessed.

The original 50–50 assumption is no longer valid.

---

# From Mathematics to Inference

Bridge decisions evolve throughout the hand.

### Before Play

Use:

- Percentage tables.
- Suit distributions.
- Honor probabilities.

### During Play

Use:

- Counting.
- Vacant Spaces.
- Restricted Choice.
- Signals.
- Revealed distribution.

The further the hand progresses, the more important inference becomes.

---

# Relationship to Conditional Probability

Conditional probability is the mathematical process that transforms **a priori** estimates into **a posteriori** probabilities.

Every new piece of evidence changes the probability of unseen cards.

For example:

- A defender opens the bidding.
- A defender shows out.
- An honor appears.
- A defender follows with an unexpected card.

Each event updates the probability of future events.

---

# Relationship to Restricted Choice

Restricted Choice begins with an a priori assumption.

After one defender plays an honor, the probability changes.

The revised estimate is an **a posteriori probability** based on newly observed evidence.

---

# Relationship to Vacant Spaces

Initially:

Each defender has an equal chance of holding a missing queen.

Later:

One defender is shown to have six spades.

The other defender now has more **vacant spaces**.

The probability of holding the queen changes.

Again, the estimate has moved from **a priori** to **a posteriori**.

---

# Example 4 – Combining Information

Suppose you need to locate:

```
♥Q
```

Initially:

- Either defender may hold it.

Later you discover:

- West opened the bidding.
- West has six clubs.
- East shows out in diamonds.

The correct decision is no longer based on the original odds but on the accumulated evidence from the auction and play.

---

# Practical Applications

Expert players update probabilities when deciding:

- Whether to finesse.
- Whether to play for the drop.
- Which defender holds a missing honor.
- Whether to draw trumps immediately.
- Which suit to establish.
- Which opponent is dangerous.

Every trick provides additional information that may change the best line of play.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Continuing to rely on initial percentages after new information appears.
- Ignoring the auction.
- Forgetting to count suit distributions.
- Ignoring Restricted Choice.
- Ignoring Vacant Spaces.
- Assuming probability remains constant throughout the hand.

---

# Summary Comparison

| A Priori | A Posteriori |
|-----------|--------------|
| Before information | After information |
| Pure mathematics | Mathematics plus evidence |
| Starting assumption | Updated estimate |
| Based on expected distributions | Based on actual observations |
| Used early in the hand | Used throughout the auction and play |

# Key Principles

- A priori probabilities provide the starting point for bridge decisions.
- A posteriori probabilities incorporate information revealed during the auction and play.
- Every bid and every played card can change the odds.
- Expert players continually revise their expectations as new evidence appears.
- The strongest bridge decisions combine mathematical probability with counting, inference, and observation.
