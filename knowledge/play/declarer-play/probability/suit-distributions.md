# Suit Distribution Probabilities

## Overview

One of the most important skills in bridge is estimating **how the missing cards in a suit are divided between the two opponents**. Knowledge of suit distribution probabilities helps declarer and defenders choose the line of play with the highest percentage of success.

Although the actual distribution on any deal is unknown, some splits occur much more frequently than others. Expert players begin with these **a priori probabilities** and then refine them using information from the auction, card play, and counting.

---

# Why Suit Distributions Matter

Suit distribution probabilities influence decisions such as:

- Whether to draw trumps immediately.
- Whether to establish a long suit.
- Whether to take a finesse.
- Whether to play for the drop.
- How many losers are likely.
- Which defender is more likely to hold a missing honor.

Understanding likely suit breaks is fundamental to percentage play.

| Missing Cards | Most Likely Split | Approximate Probability |
|--------------:|-------------------|------------------------:|
| 2 | 1–1 | 52% |
| 3 | 2–1 | 78% |
| 4 | 2–2 | 40% |
|  | 3–1 | 50% |
| 5 | 3–2 | 68% |
|  | 4–1 | 28% |

These values are rounded and are intended for practical bridge decisions.

---

# Two Missing Cards

Example:

```
You hold:
A K Q J 5

Dummy:
10 9 8 7
```

The defenders hold two cards.

Most likely split:

```
1–1
```

This occurs slightly more than half the time.

---

# Three Missing Cards

Most likely split:

```
2–1
```

approximately **78%**.

This is why declarer normally assumes a 2–1 break unless there is evidence to the contrary.

---

# Four Missing Cards

Possible splits:

```
2–2
3–1
4–0
```

Approximate frequencies:

| Split | Probability |
|--------|------------:|
| 2–2 | 40% |
| 3–1 | 50% |
| 4–0 | 10% |

Although many players hope for a 2–2 split, the **3–1 break is actually more common**.

---

# Five Missing Cards

Possible splits:

```
3–2
4–1
5–0
```

Approximate frequencies:

| Split | Probability |
|--------|------------:|
| 3–2 | 68% |
| 4–1 | 28% |
| 5–0 | 4% |

This is one of the most frequently used probability tables in bridge.

---

# Six Missing Cards

Possible splits:

```
3–3
4–2
5–1
6–0
```

Approximate frequencies:

| Split | Probability |
|--------|------------:|
| 4–2 | 48% |
| 3–3 | 36% |
| 5–1 | 15% |
| 6–0 | 1% |

Declarer must often decide whether to rely on a 3–3 break or adopt another line of play.

---

# Seven Missing Cards

Possible splits:

```
4–3
5–2
6–1
7–0
```

Approximate frequencies:

| Split | Probability |
|--------|------------:|
| 4–3 | 62% |
| 5–2 | 31% |
| 6–1 | 6% |
| 7–0 | <1% |

---

# Eight Missing Cards

Possible splits:

```
4–4
5–3
6–2
7–1
8–0
```

Approximate frequencies:

| Split | Probability |
|--------|------------:|
| 5–3 | 47% |
| 4–4 | 41% |
| 6–2 | 10% |
| 7–1 | 2% |
| 8–0 | <1% |

Many players are surprised that a **5–3 break is slightly more likely than 4–4**.

---

# Applying Distribution Probabilities

Distribution probabilities help answer questions such as:

- Will the trumps split?
- Can my long suit be established?
- Should I play for a 3–2 break or take a finesse?
- Is a safety play justified?

Good declarers compare alternative lines of play rather than relying on a single favorable break.

---

# Updating the Odds

The percentages above apply **only before any information is known**.

Once the auction or play reveals additional information, the probabilities change.

Examples:

- A defender opens the bidding.
- A defender shows out.
- A defender reveals a long suit.
- Carding reveals additional distribution.

These situations require **conditional probability** rather than raw percentages.

---

# Counting During the Play

As defenders follow suit, declarer should count:

- Cards already played.
- Remaining cards.
- Likely original distribution.

Eventually, declarer may know the exact distribution rather than relying on probabilities.

---

# Example 1

Missing five trumps.

Declarer needs trumps to split.

The expected break:

```
3–2
```

approximately **68%**.

---

# Example 2

Missing six cards in a side suit.

Declarer needs the suit to run.

The chance of a 3–3 break is only about **36%**, so another plan may be needed if the contract depends on that split.

---

# Example 3

During the play, West shows out on the second round of diamonds.

The original probability table no longer applies.

Declarer should now use counting and conditional probability to determine the remaining distribution.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Assuming all suit splits are equally likely.
- Forgetting that 3–1 is more common than 2–2.
- Forgetting that 5–3 is slightly more common than 4–4.
- Continuing to rely on raw percentages after new information appears.
- Ignoring the auction when estimating suit breaks.

# Key Principles

- Suit distributions are **not equally likely**.
- Learn the common split probabilities, especially for four, five, and six missing cards.
- Use these probabilities as the starting point for percentage play.
- Update your expectations as the auction and card play reveal additional information.
- Counting eventually replaces probability with certainty.
