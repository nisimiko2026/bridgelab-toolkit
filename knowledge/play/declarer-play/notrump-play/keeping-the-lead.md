# Keeping the Lead

## Overview

In many notrump contracts, declarer succeeds by **keeping the lead in the correct hand** while developing winners. Losing the lead at the wrong moment may allow the defenders to establish and cash their long suits before declarer has completed the plan.

The objective is not simply to win tricks, but to **maintain control of the play** until the contract is secure.

Keeping the lead is closely related to **timing**, **entry management**, and the concept of the **dangerous opponent**.

---

# Why Keeping the Lead Matters

Maintaining the lead allows declarer to:

- Continue establishing long suits.
- Preserve the initiative.
- Prevent defenders from attacking their long suits.
- Reach established winners.
- Delay defenders from gaining the lead until it is safe.

Every time declarer loses the lead, the defenders gain an opportunity to improve their position.

---

# When Keeping the Lead Is Important

Keeping the lead is especially important when:

- Defenders have an established long suit.
- Declarer still needs to establish winners.
- Entries are limited.
- One defender is particularly dangerous.
- Losing the lead would allow the defense to cash enough tricks to defeat the contract.

---

# Planning Before Trick One

Before playing from dummy, ask:

- How many times must I lose the lead?
- When is the safest time to do so?
- Which defender should win the trick?
- Can I complete my plan before giving up the lead?

Good declarers try to lose the lead only when necessary.

---

# Delaying the Loss of the Lead

Sometimes declarer should postpone a finesse or the establishment of a long suit until other winners have been cashed.

Example:

Instead of immediately losing a club trick to establish the suit, declarer first cashes all certain winners.

Even if the defenders later gain the lead, fewer tricks remain for them to attack.

---

# Choosing Which Opponent Wins

Sometimes losing the lead is unavoidable.

When possible, declarer should choose **which defender** wins the trick.

Example:

A finesse can be taken through either defender.

If losing to West allows West to cash an established suit, while losing to East is harmless, declarer should finesse through East.

This is an application of the **Dangerous Opponent** principle.

---

# Keeping the Lead and Long Suits

While establishing a long suit:

1. Cash immediate winners.
2. Preserve entries.
3. Lose the lead only when necessary.
4. Regain the lead.
5. Cash the established winners.

Careful timing minimizes the defenders' opportunities.

---

# Hold-Up Play

A hold-up play deliberately loses the lead—but at the **right time**.

By ducking the defenders' long suit, declarer breaks their communication before eventually winning the trick.

Although declarer temporarily gives up the lead, the overall strategy helps maintain control of the hand.

---

# Entries and Communication

Keeping the lead depends on preserving communication.

Avoid:

- Using critical entries too early.
- Blocking established suits.
- Reaching the wrong hand.

Good entry management allows declarer to continue attacking without surrendering the initiative.

---

# Example 1 – Cash Winners First

Contract:

```
3NT
```

Declarer has eight immediate winners and must establish one club trick.

Rather than immediately giving up a club, declarer first cashes all eight winners.

Only then does declarer lose the lead to establish clubs.

The defenders have less time to develop their own tricks.

---

# Example 2 – Choosing the Safe Finesse

Declarer can finesse either opponent for the queen of diamonds.

West has led spades and appears to have a long spade suit.

Losing to East is much safer.

Declarer therefore chooses the finesse through East.

---

# Example 3 – Preserving the Initiative

Declarer must choose between:

- Taking an immediate finesse.
- Establishing a long suit later.

The finesse risks losing the lead too early.

Declarer first develops certain winners before attempting the finesse.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Losing the lead before completing the declarer's plan.
- Taking unnecessary early finesses.
- Ignoring the dangerous opponent.
- Establishing a long suit without preserving entries.
- Failing to cash immediate winners first.
- Giving the defenders multiple opportunities to attack.

# Key Principles

- Keep the lead until you have completed as much of your plan as possible.
- Lose the lead only when it is necessary and, if possible, to the safer defender.
- Cash immediate winners before giving the defenders an opportunity to attack.
- Preserve entries and communication so you can maintain the initiative.
- Good timing and careful planning often determine when declarer should surrender the lead.
